1Business basics

Do these before you take money from anyone. Rules differ by state and country, so check locally: a local small-business development centre, a free mentoring service (in the US, the SBA and SCORE), or an accountant can tell you what applies to you in one meeting.

  • Name. Pick a business and product name; check nobody else uses it for software (search the trademark register) and that the .com is free (section 2).
  • Register the business (sole proprietor or a limited company / LLC). An LLC separates your personal assets from the business; ask an accountant which suits you.
  • Tax ID (in the US an EIN, free from the IRS) and a business bank account. Keep business money separate from day one.
  • Bookkeeping: a simple accounting app or spreadsheet for income, expenses and the server bills.
  • Insurance quotes: general liability; professional liability (errors and omissions), because shops will make ordering, pricing and allergen decisions with your software; and cyber liability, because you'll hold their sales, staff and pay data.
  • Getting paid: invoices that accept card and bank transfer, with automatic monthly billing for subscriptions.
Not legal or tax advice This plan lists what to sort out; a local professional should tell you how.

2Your domain and server

One domain for everything: a subdomain per shop

Yes: buy one domain for your brand, and use it for the demo and for every customer. The app runs one shop per copy, so each shop gets its own copy at its own subdomain, with its own data, logins and backups. One shop can never see another's data, and a problem in one doesn't touch the rest.

AddressWhat it's for
demo.yourbrand.comSales demos: the sample juice bar, with demo logins, put back to the sample every night.
sunrise.yourbrand.com, greenbar.yourbrand.com …One per paying (or pilot) shop. Starts empty; you load their menu.
yourbrand.comOptional: a one-page website with the brochure and your contact details.
you@yourbrand.comBusiness email on your domain. Customers and suppliers take it more seriously than a free address.

2.1 Buy the domain

  • Short, easy to spell when said out loud, ideally .com. Any well-known registrar works (Cloudflare, Namecheap and Porkbun are common choices).
  • Turn on auto-renew, registrar lock and two-factor sign-in on the registrar account. Losing the domain takes every customer offline.

2.2 Rent a small server

  • A Linux virtual server (Ubuntu LTS) with 2 GB of memory from any mainstream provider (DigitalOcean, Hetzner, Linode/Akamai, Vultr, AWS Lightsail…).
  • Each shop uses about 50 MB of memory (measured), so a 2 GB server should run the demo plus roughly 15–20 small shops. Watch memory and upgrade when it gets tight.
  • Secure it on day one: sign in with SSH keys (not passwords), a firewall that only allows ports 22, 80 and 443, automatic security updates, and two-factor sign-in on the hosting account.

2.3 Point the domain at the server

In your registrar's DNS settings, add two A records pointing at the server's IP address: one for demo, and a wildcard *. With the wildcard, new shops need no DNS changes.

2.4 Start the demo

Install Docker on the server, copy this project onto it, put your domain into deploy/multi/Caddyfile, then:

cd deploy/multi
docker compose up -d --build

Caddy gets the HTTPS certificate by itself within a minute or two. The demo starts with the sample juice bar.

2.5 Create the accounts straight away

Do this before you share any link On a brand-new copy, whoever opens it first can create the owner account. So create it yourself the moment a copy starts:
docker compose exec demo python -m app.auth add yourname owner       # asks for a password
docker compose exec demo python -m app.auth add demo-manager manager
docker compose exec demo python -m app.auth add demo-staff staff

Give your salesperson the demo-manager and demo-staff logins, never your owner login.

2.6 Reset the demo every night

So every demo starts clean, whatever was clicked the day before. On the server, run crontab -e and add:

15 3 * * *  /path/to/project/deploy/multi/reset-demo.sh >> /var/log/menu-demo-reset.log 2>&1

Demo logins and passwords are kept. Run the same script by hand just before an important demo.

2.7 Backups and monitoring

  • Turn on the hosting provider's weekly server snapshots.
  • Every week, download each shop's full backup (Settings → Backups) and keep it off the server. Shops also get automatic daily copies on the server.
  • Add each address to an uptime monitor (several have free plans) so you hear about an outage before your customers do.
  • Once, practise a restore on the demo (python -m app.backup restore …; see docs/HOSTING.md) so you know it works.

3Test it like a customer

Before anyone else sees it, use the live demo exactly as a shop would. Fix or note anything that surprises you.

  • On your phone, on mobile data (not your Wi-Fi), open https://demo.yourbrand.com. Check the padlock shows and the page loads quickly.
  • Sign in as staff and do every task in the Staff guide: a sale, an allergy check, a delivery with one damaged item, a count, a waste entry, sign out.
  • Sign in as manager and do every task in the Manager guide: reset a staff password, import a till file, create a purchase order, move a shift, read the variance report.
  • Take both knowledge checks yourself without looking. Anything you get wrong, a customer will too: improve the guide.
  • Get a real till export (from your pilot shop, a friend's café, or a free trial of Square, Toast or Clover) and import it. Check the columns and names match.
  • Load a real menu through Excel: download the workbook from an empty copy, fill in Ingredients, Menu Items and Recipes, import it. Time how long it takes: that's your onboarding effort per shop.
  • Restore a backup onto the demo (section 2.7).
  • Try it on the tablet a shop would actually use at the counter and at the back door.

4Pilot with one real shop

This is the most important step. A real shop's results are what you (and later your salesperson) will sell, in place of the illustrative estimate in the proposal.

Choose the shop

  • Makes drinks or food to recipes from ingredients it buys (juice bar, smoothie or coffee shop, bakery café, small kitchen).
  • One location; uses a till system that exports item sales to CSV.
  • An owner who already feels the pain: waste, running out, not knowing their drink costs, ordering from memory.

Offer and agree

  • Free or heavily discounted for 6–8 weeks, in exchange for honest feedback, a weekly 20-minute check-in, a testimonial, and permission to use their before/after numbers (anonymously if they prefer).
  • Put it in a one-page pilot agreement (section 5): dates, what you do, what they do, that their data is theirs, what happens at the end.

Measure before, then after

MeasureHow to get it before the pilot
Food cost %Last month's ingredient purchases ÷ sales (from invoices and the till).
WasteAsk them to note what they throw away for one week.
Stock-outsHow many times a week something runs out ("86'd").
Time on adminHours a week spent counting, ordering and scheduling.

At the end, compare, write a one-page case study, and update the proposal and brochure with the real figures.

Set up a shop (repeat for every customer)

  1. In deploy/multi/docker-compose.yml, copy the commented customer template block; rename it (e.g. sunrise) and its volume.
  2. In the Caddyfile, copy the commented block with the same name. Run docker compose up -d.
  3. Immediately create your owner account and the shop owner's account (section 2.5, with sunrise in place of demo).
  4. Collect: menu and recipes, recent supplier invoices, staff list with pay rates, a sample till export.
  5. Load the menu through the Excel template (section 3); set reorder points, par levels, yields and allergens; add staff; set the target food cost.
  6. Train the owner with the Manager guide and staff with the Staff guide; have everyone take their knowledge check.
  7. Check in weekly for the first month.

When a shop leaves: download their full backup and give it to them (it's their data), then remove their block and volume.

5Package, price and paperwork

Decide the offer

A one-time setup fee (your onboarding time) plus a monthly fee (hosting, backups, updates, support) is easiest to explain. Keep what's included realistic for one person, as you did with the brochure: write it down so nobody, including a salesperson, promises more.

  • Setup: decide what's included, e.g. "we load up to 40 menu items from your completed template and train your team in one session". Anything beyond is extra.
  • Support: how customers reach you, your hours, and how fast you reply (e.g. "next business day").
  • Monthly price floor: (your support hours per shop per month × your hourly rate) + server share + payment fees, then add a margin. Example: 2 hours × $40 + $2 server + $3 fees = $85 cost, so price comfortably above that.

Documents to have ready

DocumentCovers
Service agreementWhat you provide; fees and billing; term and cancellation; support hours and reply times; best-effort availability; the customer owns their data and gets a full export on leaving; backups; limit of liability; that costs, reorder suggestions and allergen lists depend on what the shop enters and don't replace their own allergen procedures.
Pilot agreementA one-page version for the free or discounted trial (section 4).
Privacy noticeWhat personal data you hold (staff names, contact details, pay rates, sign-in records), why, where it's stored, who can see it, how long you keep it.
Salesperson agreementCommission terms, what they may and may not promise, confidentiality, ending the arrangement (section 7).

Pay a lawyer once to review these as templates; it's far cheaper than fixing a dispute. Then update the proposal's pricing table to match.

6Sales kit

Have all of this ready before you hire, so the salesperson sells the same thing you would.

  • Brochure with your real contact details (fill in the placeholders in docs/brochure/brochure.html), printed on good paper.
  • Proposal template updated with the pilot's real results and your price sheet.
  • Case study from the pilot: one page, before/after numbers, a quote from the owner.
  • Demo logins for the salesperson (demo-manager and demo-staff), and a tablet to demo on.
  • Demo script (below), qualifying questions and answers to objections (next page).
  • A simple pipeline sheet: shop, contact, till system, stage (contacted, demo booked, demo done, pilot, customer), next step and date.
  • Two short follow-up emails: one the same day as a demo, one a week later.

The 10-minute demo

TimeShowSay (in your words)
0–1Ask, don't show"How do you know what each drink costs you today? What happens when you run out of something mid-shift?"
1–3Menu builder: open a drink"Here's what this smoothie really costs, ingredient by ingredient, and the price that hits your target. Allergens come from the recipe."
3–4Till import"Each morning you load yesterday's sales from your till. Nobody types anything twice."
4–6Dashboard → Procurement"It tells you what's running low, before it runs out, and builds the order for each supplier."
6–7Receiving"Staff check deliveries off on the tablet: damaged or missing items are recorded, not paid for by surprise."
7–8Counts & Waste"A weekly count shows what went missing, in dollars."
8–9Schedule"Drag shifts around and see labor against sales."
9–10Settings → Users; Excel"Everyone has their own login; it's backed up daily; and it's all in Excel, which you keep." Then ask for a pilot or a follow-up.

Qualifying questions (is this shop a fit?)

  • What do you sell that's made from ingredients you buy? Roughly how many items on the menu?
  • Which till system do you use, and can it export item sales? (Square, Toast, Clover and most others can.)
  • How do you order today, and how often do you run out or throw things away?
  • How many staff, and how do you do the schedule?
  • Who would use it day to day, and on what device?

Objections and honest answers

They sayYou say
"My till already does inventory.""Most track items sold, not the ingredients inside them. This works out what each drink used, what to reorder from which supplier, and what went missing."
"We use spreadsheets.""This keeps a live Excel copy for you, but does the arithmetic after every sale, delivery and count, so nobody has to."
"There are bigger products for this.""Yes, built for bigger operations and priced like it. This is made for one or two shops: simple, quick to set up, and your data stays yours in Excel."
"I don't have time to set it up.""You fill in a template with your menu and suppliers; we load it and train your team in one session."
"Is my data safe?""Your shop runs on its own, separate from every other customer, over HTTPS. Everyone has their own login, it's backed up daily, and you can download everything any time."
"What about allergies?""It lists the allergens in every drink from its recipe, so staff can answer quickly. It doesn't know about shared blenders, so it supports your allergy procedure; it doesn't replace it."
"It's too expensive."Use the pilot's real numbers: what the shop saved in waste and stock-outs each month against the monthly fee.

7Recruit and train a salesperson

Who to look for

Someone who knows the local café and restaurant scene (a former manager, a supplier rep, a hospitality consultant), is comfortable walking in to introduce themselves, and listens more than they talk.

How to pay them

  • Commission-only (a share of each new customer's setup fee and first months of subscription) costs nothing until they sell; a small base plus commission attracts steadier people.
  • Pay commission when the customer pays, and take it back if the customer cancels within the first 60–90 days.
  • Check whether they're a contractor or an employee where you are (the rules matter for tax), and put it in a written agreement.

Rules of engagement (write them down)

  • Sell only what's on the price sheet; no discounts or custom features without you.
  • No promised start dates; you confirm onboarding dates.
  • Use the allergen wording above; never say a drink is "allergen-free".
  • They find, qualify, demo and sign; you set up the shop and train it. Handoff: contact, till system, menu size, locations, pain points, hoped-for start date.

Two-week training plan

WhenWhat

Days 3–5Shadow at least two of your demos. Learn the qualifying questions and the 10-minute script.
